Channels, like networks, are used to move data between a host, such as your PC and a peripheral device, such as your subsystem. There are significant differences between channels and networks, however, as described in the table below.

Terminology

Data is moved over Fibre Channel in the form of frames. A frame is data unit of up to 2048 bytes, encapsulated with a header that contains routing information and a trailer that contains control and error detection data.

A Fibre Channel device is called a node. The device may be a PC with a Fibre Channel HBA card, a subsystem, or some other device. The node communicates through a port, a pair of fibers, either optical cable or elecrical wires. One of the fibers carries information into the port, the other carries information out. The fiber pair is called a link. The link connects the node with other devices on the topology.

There are three common types of topology in the Fibre Channel world, point-to-point, arbitrated loop, and fabric.

Point-to-point is the simplest topology. It consists of a single, duplex connection between two nodes. An example of this arrangement is Direct Attached Storage (DAS), where a PC with a Fibre Channel HBA card connects directly to the data ports on a subsystem.

Arbitrated loop is a system of three to 127 nodes connected in a loop, similar to a token-ring networking protocol. Each port sees all the frames (messages) travelling on the loop but only pays attention to those frames addressed that port. Aribitrated loop is an inexpensive way to connect multiple nodes. However, bandwidth of the loop is shared, only two ports are active at one time, with the other ports acting as repeaters.

Fabric is a system using dynamic interconnection between nodes, achieved by means of a switch or a router. Any node can communicate with any other node at any time. The switch or router handles frame routing, so only the target port sees the frames. A fabric supports both peer-to-peer (network) and master-slave (channel) communication. A fabric can be easily expanded by adding more switches or nodes, up to a total of 16 million ports logged in at the same time.

A device that makes a request of another is called the initiator. The device responding to the request is called the target. For example, you are working at your PC and want to access files on your subsystem. When you go to open the logical drive directory, your PC sends the request to the and the responds. In this case, the PC is the initiator and the is the target. Initiators and targets are identified by a double 64-bit World Wide Name (WWN). This WWN includes a unique manufacturer identifier and a unique port identifier.

Troubleshooting

Fibre Channel is a well-proven and reliable method of data communication. As with any system, however, problems can arise. When there is a connection failure, use to verify that sees the initiator(s). If sees some initiators but not the one you want, the problem is most likely elsewhere in the loop or fabric. If does not see any initiators:

  • Check all of the Fibre Channel connections
  • Verify that all nodes are properly connected and powered
  • Verify that the fabric router or switch is properly connected powered

The Fibre Channel ports, transceivers, and controller on function the same as comparable components on other nodes. Note that 's management functions take place over a separate IP network connection. Therefore it is possible to have access to the via CLU over Telnet or even though the Fibre Channel connection is offline.
